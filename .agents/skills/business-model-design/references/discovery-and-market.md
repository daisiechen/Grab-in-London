# Discovery and Market

## Problems and needs

Describe the trigger, job to be done, current behavior, obstacle, consequence and frequency. Separate functional, emotional and social needs. Theory organizes questions; it does not replace behavioral evidence. Personal experience is a lead, not prevalence data.

Ask about recent actual experiences, money/time already spent, workarounds and abandonment. Include users, non-users and lost users; record recruitment bias. Separate expressed interest, survey intent, payment and continued use. If interviews are unavailable, make a bounded assessment from verifiable material and identify what needs primary validation.

## Segments and value

Segment by task, obstacle, urgency, budget, buying authority and alternative. Demographics help test differences but do not automatically explain needs. Separate target customers, early adopters, seed-user channels and payers.

For each participant, specify the outcome received, cost borne, reason to participate and reason to leave. Validate each side of a multi-party business. Accessible channels do not establish a large market. State positioning as a particular audience obtaining a particular outcome in a particular situation; add mechanism only when useful.

## Sizing

Define geography, time, population, job and unit before calculating. Distinguish people/organizations, demand occasions, spending pools, obtainable revenue and delivery capacity.

Use the simplest auditable method: units x frequency x payable share x price, or a reliable aggregate filtered by explicit criteria. Cross-check without adding overlapping populations or incompatible periods.

If using TAM/SAM/SOM, define total opportunity, service boundary and attainable scope. SOM must reflect channel access, competition, time and delivery capacity. With insufficient inputs, provide ranges, conditional calculations or missing inputs rather than arbitrary precision.

Separate ability to pay from willingness to pay. Household spending, enterprise budgets and industry turnover do not directly establish an individual's price or subscription budget. Record year, sample, definitions and proxy limitations.

## Alternatives and competition

Compare direct competitors, indirect substitutes, manual services, existing habits and doing nothing. Evaluate task outcomes, total cost, control, trust, switching effort and distribution, not just features.

Verify region, currency, billing basis, free boundaries and delivery responsibilities. Attribute vendor claims and disclose missing denominators. Missing public metrics do not establish failure. Separate potential advantages from demonstrated advantages and explain why customers might switch.

Completion: identify where the problem holds, for whom, the remaining gap in alternatives, the evidence behind market estimates and uncertainties that could change the solution.
