# Business Model and Economics

## Solution alternatives

Compare plausible products, services, manual processes and combinations against needs. Explain outcomes, user effort, mechanisms, operational dependencies, costs and tradeoffs. For an operating business, analyze the current state and proposed changes rather than restarting discovery unnecessarily.

Trust, safety, quality, fulfillment, data permissions and technology belong in the solution and delivery model, with detail proportional to risk. Technology requires a concrete benefit, alternative baseline and cost justification. AI and better UX do not automatically constitute a moat.

## Monetization

Specify who pays, why, what is delivered, billing unit, price rationale, costs and cancellation/refund obligations. Demonstrate value separately when users and payers differ.

- Explain the purpose and cost of free service. Paid tiers buy additional delivery, allowance, rights or outcomes, not merely a status label.
- Subscription requires repeated value and ongoing delivery. Compare per-use, usage-based, commission, project, licensing and hybrid models against task frequency and fulfillment.
- Distinguish transaction value, pass-through funds, deposits, platform revenue, subsidies and taxes according to the commercial relationship.
- Multi-sided businesses must examine incentive conflicts, incrementality, attribution, alternatives and double charging.
- Competitor prices inform boundaries; actual purchasing, costs and pricing power still need evidence.

## Operations and growth

Connect acquisition, activation, supply, delivery, repeat use, churn and support. Allow episodic or low-frequency needs and successful departure; measure retention on a window appropriate to the job.

Check local supply/demand density, availability, waits, cancellations and capacity. Signed supply is not available supply. Count manual effort separately and distinguish concierge work, tools and measured automation. Expansion depends on repeatable acquisition and delivery, not registration totals.

## Economic model

Choose a model proportionate to the decision. Label inputs as observed, benchmark, proxy or assumption; preserve formulas, ranges and sensitive drivers.

Define the economic unit: customer, order, event, seat, task or another relevant unit. Include volume/price, discounts, refunds, service, labor, infrastructure, acquisition and applicable safety/compliance costs. Free users and failed transactions may also cost money.

Separate contribution, fixed costs, cash flow, accounting profit, taxes and financing. Steady-state scenarios are not growth forecasts. Budget/fundraising models should account for startup costs, ramp, working capital and runway.

Define CAC by new-customer cohort, channel and included costs. Use LTV only with supportable observation periods and retention assumptions. Do not transfer benchmarks across incompatible payers, regions, stages or delivery models, or use future revenue to hide present delivery losses.

Reconcile volume and price, supply constraints, break-even and sensitivity. Negative unit contribution cannot be repaired merely by increasing volume. Change representative inputs and verify dependent calculations; keep a reproducible model.

Completion: charges correspond to delivery, participants have credible incentives, economics and necessary conditions are traceable, and unknowns remain explicit.
